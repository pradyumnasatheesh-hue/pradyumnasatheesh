Case study. Hyderabad EXO and the ONE card, a systems thinking project

A city of 11 million, one guide to what makes it itself, and one card to get you there.

EXO started as a concept in a systems thinking project. I rebuilt it as a full product: a design system, 31 app screens, and a transit card designed as a product of its own.

Where it started

Six of us asked what makes a city smart. We said culture.

Six of us spent two to four weeks on one question: what makes a city smart? Our answer was culture, not technology. Two of our four concepts answered that directly. EXO, a guide to the city's experiences. ONE, a single mobility card. This page is my rebuild of those two as one product.

Thinking in systems

Before a product, a system.

The project was our introduction to thinking in systems. We did not start from an app. We started from the city, and asked where a small push would move the most.

1
We mapped everyone a smart city touches

A city is not smart because of how much technology it has. It is smart when that technology makes the city work better for the people in it.

The stakeholder map, redrawn by hand from our original. Six groups, and culture is one of them.
2
We found two cities that never fully became one

The old city holds the heritage, craft and food. The new city is IT parks and a more Western way of living. Fieldwork showed how rarely the two meet.

Hyderabad's culture today, from our fieldwork notes.

Although the twin cities have co-existed as one for decades, there is a major alienation of cultural demographics between the new and old areas.

the gap that shaped everything after it
3
We ranked every idea on Donella Meadows' leverage points

Meadows lists twelve places to intervene in a system, from changing a number to changing its goal. The lower the number, the more of the system it moves. We placed each of our six opportunities on that ladder.

    How the rebuild keeps thinking in systems
    Most of what I added works on Meadows' point 6, information flows: putting information where it was missing.
    • Who vetted a place, and why it ranks where it does, shown on the screen.
    • The fare for every leg, shown inside the plan.
    • Money that has not reached the card yet, shown as "Waiting to load".

    The card as a product

    A card has no screen. So I designed everything around it.

    Rs 500 loadedBalance Rs 920

    Modern card rules I held to
    • Numberless face. The full number is never printed or shown.
    • Portrait, because that is how a card is held at a gate.
    • One colour per tier, taken from the app's five materials.
    • A notch at the reader end that fingers can find.
    Three forms, one account
    Physical cardBalance on the chip. Works with no signal.
    In-app cardThe Pass tab is the wallet: load, freeze, alerts, history.
    Scan passA refreshing code for visitors and forgotten cards.
    The constraint that shaped it most
    On India's common mobility cards, money added online reaches the chip only at the next tap. Most apps hide that. I made it a screen.

    So Pass shows two balances, "On the card" and "Waiting to load", and the gate confirms the moment it lands.

    Fixed by how the standard works
    • A RuPay prepaid card with a stored balance on the chip.
    • That balance is what a gate reads, so taps work offline.
    • Apple Wallet is not an option for this card today.
    Assumed, to be validated
    • Gates and buses with code scanners.
    • Reading the card over NFC on Android.
    • Tier names, pass lengths and every price.
    What I took away

    A product is the screens plus everything that happens when there is no screen.

    The app was the easy half. The card forced me to design a counter, a gate, a sound and a refund. Next I would test two things with riders in Hyderabad: whether "Waiting to load" is understood on first read, and whether a curator's name earns more trust than a star rating.
